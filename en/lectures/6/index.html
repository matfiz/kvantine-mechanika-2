6.1 Resolvent and spectrum

In Quantum Mechanics I, Postulate 4.1, the readings of an observable AAA are its eigenvalues, the numbers λ\lambdaλ for which the matrix A−λA-\lambdaA−λ has no inverse. The position QQQ on the line has no eigenvalue (Theorem 4.6), yet every interval of positive length is read with positive probability in some state. The test that still works asks for a bounded inverse. Here and below, A−zA-zA−z stands for the operator A−z11A-z\IdA−z11 on D(A)\dom AD(A).

Definition 6.1 (Resolvent and spectrum). Let AAA be an operator on H\HilH. A complex number zzz lies in the resolvent set of AAA if A−zA-zA−z maps D(A)\dom AD(A) one-to-one onto H\HilH and its inverse (A−z)−1(A-z)^{-1}(A−z)−1 is bounded. This inverse is the resolvent of AAA at zzz. The spectrum σ(A)\sigma(A)σ(A) is the set of complex numbers that do not lie in the resolvent set.

For example, let A=c11A=c\IdA=c11 on H≠{0}\Hil\ne\{0\}H={0}, with c∈Rc\in\Rc∈R. If z≠cz\ne cz=c, then (A−z)−1=(c−z)−111(A-z)^{-1}=(c-z)^{-1}\Id(A−z)−1=(c−z)−111 and its norm is 1/∣c−z∣1/\abs{c-z}1/∣c−z∣. At z=cz=cz=c the operator A−cA-cA−c is zero, so σ(A)={c}\sigma(A)=\{c\}σ(A)={c}. The inverse becomes unbounded as zzz approaches the one possible reading ccc.

If AAA is bounded and D(A)=H\dom A=\HilD(A)=H, this is the spectrum of Linear Algebra, Def. 14.7. Every eigenvalue λ\lambdaλ of AAA lies in σ(A)\sigma(A)σ(A), since A−λA-\lambdaA−λ maps an eigenvector to 000 and is not one-to-one. For zzz in the resolvent set and every φ∈H\varphi\in\Hilφ∈H, the vector ψ=(A−z)−1φ\psi=(A-z)^{-1}\varphiψ=(A−z)−1φ is the one solution in D(A)\dom AD(A) of the equation (A−z)ψ=φ(A-z)\psi=\varphi(A−z)ψ=φ. An eigenvector shows that a number lies in the spectrum. Without one, approximate eigenvectors do.

For an eigenvalue, a unit eigenvector, repeated, is a Weyl sequence. For a self-adjoint operator, every point of the spectrum has a Weyl sequence. This is part of the main theorem of this section. We state it now and prove it after the two lemmas that it needs.

Theorem 6.3 (The spectrum of a self-adjoint operator). Let AAA be self-adjoint.

  1. The spectrum σ(A)\sigma(A)σ(A) lies in R\RR, and ∥(A−z)−1∥≤1/∣Im⁡z∣\norm{(A-z)^{-1}}\le1/\abs{\operatorname{Im}z}​(A−z)−1​≤1/∣Imz∣ for every non-real zzz.

  2. Weyl’s criterion. A number λ\lambdaλ lies in σ(A)\sigma(A)σ(A) if and only if it has a Weyl sequence.

  3. The spectrum σ(A)\sigma(A)σ(A) is closed.

So λ\lambdaλ lies in the spectrum exactly when there are states on which A−λA-\lambdaA−λ is as small as one likes.

Summary

  • Resolvent and spectrum (Definition 6.1). A Weyl sequence for λ\lambdaλ puts λ\lambdaλ in the spectrum (Proposition 6.2). The spectrum of a self-adjoint operator is real and closed, and it consists of the points with Weyl sequences (Theorem 6.3); by a cited theorem, it holds the readings (Theorem 6.4). Along a Weyl sequence for a real λ\lambdaλ, the readings converge in probability to λ\lambdaλ. A symmetric operator with A−zA-zA−z and A−z‾A-\overline zA−z onto H\HilH for one non-real zzz is self-adjoint (Proposition 6.7).
  • Spectra by hand: a diagonal operator, and multiplication by a real continuous function, have the closure of their values as spectrum (Proposition 6.8).
  • Kinds of spectrum: point and continuous, discrete and essential (Definition 6.9); a point is essential exactly when it has an orthonormal Weyl sequence (Theorem 6.10, cited), and an interval of positive length inside the spectrum is essential.
  • The free resolvent has the kernel (m/ℏ2η)e−η∣x−y∣(m/\hbar^2\eta)e^{-\eta\abs{x-y}}(m/ℏ2η)e−η∣x−y∣ at z=−ℏ2η2/2mz=-\hbar^2\eta^2/2mz=−ℏ2η2/2m (Proposition 6.11), and the infinite chain has the band [c−2κ,c+2κ][c-2\kappa,c+2\kappa][c−2κ,c+2κ] and no eigenvalue (Proposition 6.12).
