Lecture 6

The spectrum and the resolvent

The spectrum of a self-adjoint operator is real and closed. We prove Weyl’s criterion and compute spectra by explicit inverses and approximate eigenvectors. By a cited theorem, the spectrum holds the readings. The free resolvent and infinite chain give two applications.

6.1 Resolvent and spectrum

In Quantum Mechanics I, Postulate 4.1, the readings of an observable AAA are its eigenvalues, the numbers λ\lambdaλ for which the matrix A−λA-\lambdaA−λ has no inverse. The position QQQ on the line has no eigenvalue (Theorem 4.6), yet every interval of positive length is read with positive probability in some state. The test that still works asks for a bounded inverse. Here and below, A−zA-zA−z stands for the operator A−z11A-z\IdA−z11 on D(A)\dom AD(A).

Definition 6.1 (Resolvent and spectrum). Let AAA be an operator on H\HilH. A complex number zzz lies in the resolvent set of AAA if A−zA-zA−z maps D(A)\dom AD(A) one-to-one onto H\HilH and its inverse (A−z)−1(A-z)^{-1}(A−z)−1 is bounded. This inverse is the resolvent of AAA at zzz. The spectrum σ(A)\sigma(A)σ(A) is the set of complex numbers that do not lie in the resolvent set.

For example, let A=c11A=c\IdA=c11 on H≠{0}\Hil\ne\{0\}H={0}, with c∈Rc\in\Rc∈R. If z≠cz\ne cz=c, then (A−z)−1=(c−z)−111(A-z)^{-1}=(c-z)^{-1}\Id(A−z)−1=(c−z)−111 and its norm is 1/∣c−z∣1/\abs{c-z}1/∣c−z∣. At z=cz=cz=c the operator A−cA-cA−c is zero, so σ(A)={c}\sigma(A)=\{c\}σ(A)={c}. The inverse becomes unbounded as zzz approaches the one possible reading ccc.

If AAA is bounded and D(A)=H\dom A=\HilD(A)=H, this is the spectrum of Linear Algebra, Def. 14.7. Every eigenvalue λ\lambdaλ of AAA lies in σ(A)\sigma(A)σ(A), since A−λA-\lambdaA−λ maps an eigenvector to 000 and is not one-to-one. For zzz in the resolvent set and every φ∈H\varphi\in\Hilφ∈H, the vector ψ=(A−z)−1φ\psi=(A-z)^{-1}\varphiψ=(A−z)−1φ is the one solution in D(A)\dom AD(A) of the equation (A−z)ψ=φ(A-z)\psi=\varphi(A−z)ψ=φ. An eigenvector shows that a number lies in the spectrum. Without one, approximate eigenvectors do.

For an eigenvalue, a unit eigenvector, repeated, is a Weyl sequence. For a self-adjoint operator, every point of the spectrum has a Weyl sequence. The proof needs one fact about Hilbert spaces, which Linear Algebra, Thm 13.30, cites. We prove it. A subspace MMM of H\HilH is closed if the limit of every convergent sequence in MMM lies in MMM.

Theorem 6.5 (The spectrum of a self-adjoint operator). Let AAA be self-adjoint.

  1. The spectrum σ(A)\sigma(A)σ(A) lies in R\RR, and ∥(A−z)−1∥≤1/∣Im⁡z∣\norm{(A-z)^{-1}}\le1/\abs{\operatorname{Im}z}​(A−z)−1​≤1/∣Imz∣ for every non-real zzz.

  2. Weyl’s criterion. A number λ\lambdaλ lies in σ(A)\sigma(A)σ(A) if and only if it has a Weyl sequence.

  3. The spectrum σ(A)\sigma(A)σ(A) is closed.

Proof.

  1. For non-real zzz, (6.1) gives ∥(A−z)ψ∥≥∣Im⁡z∣∥ψ∥\norm{(A-z)\psi}\ge\abs{\operatorname{Im}z}\norm\psi∥(A−z)ψ∥≥∣Imz∣∥ψ∥, and Lemma 6.4 applies with c=∣Im⁡z∣c=\abs{\operatorname{Im}z}c=∣Imz∣.

  2. By Proposition 6.2, a Weyl sequence for λ\lambdaλ puts λ\lambdaλ in σ(A)\sigma(A)σ(A). Conversely, suppose that λ\lambdaλ has no Weyl sequence. Then there is c>0c>0c>0 with ∥(A−λ)ψ∥≥c∥ψ∥\norm{(A-\lambda)\psi}\ge c\norm\psi∥(A−λ)ψ∥≥c∥ψ∥ for every ψ∈D(A)\psi\in\dom Aψ∈D(A), since otherwise for each nnn some unit vector ψn\psi_nψn​ would have ∥(A−λ)ψn∥<1/n\norm{(A-\lambda)\psi_n}<1/n∥(A−λ)ψn​∥<1/n. By Lemma 6.4, λ\lambdaλ lies in the resolvent set.

  3. Let λk∈σ(A)\lambda_k\in\sigma(A)λk​∈σ(A) converge to λ\lambdaλ. By Weyl’s criterion there are unit vectors ψk∈D(A)\psi_k\in\dom Aψk​∈D(A) with ∥(A−λk)ψk∥<1/k\norm{(A-\lambda_k)\psi_k}<1/k∥(A−λk​)ψk​∥<1/k. Then ∥(A−λ)ψk∥<1/k+∣λk−λ∣→0\norm{(A-\lambda)\psi_k}<1/k+\abs{\lambda_k-\lambda}\to0∥(A−λ)ψk​∥<1/k+∣λk​−λ∣→0, so λ∈σ(A)\lambda\in\sigma(A)λ∈σ(A).

 

So λ\lambdaλ lies in the spectrum exactly when there are states on which A−λA-\lambdaA−λ is as small as one likes (Teschl, Thm 2.18 and Lemma 2.16; Amrein, Prop. 4.20). The spectrum also holds the readings, by a theorem that needs the spectral theorem.

Theorem 6.6 (Readings lie in the spectrumcited). Let AAA be self-adjoint. A real number λ\lambdaλ lies in σ(A)\sigma(A)σ(A) if and only if EA((λ−ϵ,λ+ϵ))≠0\pvm A((\lambda-\epsilon,\lambda+\epsilon))\ne0EA​((λ−ϵ,λ+ϵ))=0 for every ϵ>0\epsilon>0ϵ>0, and EA(I)=0\pvm A(I)=0EA​(I)=0 for every interval III that contains no point of σ(A)\sigma(A)σ(A). This is Teschl, Thm 3.8 and Cor. 3.9, and Amrein, Example 4.13. By Postulate 5.5, a reading of AAA in an interval that misses σ(A)\sigma(A)σ(A) has probability 000 in every state, and a reading in an open interval around a point of σ(A)\sigma(A)σ(A) has positive probability in some state. The spectrum is the continuum’s form of the eigenvalues of Quantum Mechanics I.

Self-adjointness can be tested without computing A†\adj AA†, by solving two equations.

6.3 Kinds of spectrum

Let AAA be self-adjoint. Its eigenvalues form the point spectrum, and the other points of σ(A)\sigma(A)σ(A) form the continuous spectrum. These are the names of Linear Algebra, Def. 14.7, since for a self-adjoint AAA and a real λ\lambdaλ that is not an eigenvalue, the range of A−λA-\lambdaA−λ is dense. Indeed, as in the proof of Lemma 6.4, a vector φ\varphiφ orthogonal to the range lies in D(A)\dom AD(A) and has Aφ=λφA\varphi=\lambda\varphiAφ=λφ, so φ=0\varphi=0φ=0. Then Lemma 6.3, applied to the closure of the range, shows that the range is dense. Definition 6.9 (Discrete and essential spectrum). Let AAA be self-adjoint. A point λ∈σ(A)\lambda\in\sigma(A)λ∈σ(A) lies in the discrete spectrum σd(A)\sigma_{\mathrm d}(A)σd​(A) if it is an eigenvalue with an eigenspace of finite dimension, and some interval (λ−ϵ,λ+ϵ)(\lambda-\epsilon,\lambda+\epsilon)(λ−ϵ,λ+ϵ) contains no other point of σ(A)\sigma(A)σ(A). Theorem 6.10 (Weyl’s criterion for the essential spectrumcited). Let AAA be self-adjoint and λ\lambdaλ real. Then λ∈σess(A)\lambda\in\sigma_{\mathrm{ess}}(A)λ∈σess​(A) if and only if λ\lambdaλ has a Weyl sequence of orthonormal vectors.

Linear Algebra, Lecture 14, warns that a continuous spectrum is not a continuous distribution of readings. A diagonal operator can have continuous spectrum, though all its distributions are point weights (Exercise Sheet 6). Lecture 12 sorts states by their distributions, not by the continuous spectrum.

An eigenvalue can also lie inside an interval of essential spectrum. Put two impenetrable walls on the line, at 000 and at LLL. Between them the particle has the box levels EnE_nEn​. Summary

  • Resolvent and spectrum (Definition 6.1). A Weyl sequence for λ\lambdaλ puts λ\lambdaλ in the spectrum (Proposition 6.2). The spectrum of a self-adjoint operator is real and closed, and it consists of the points with Weyl sequences (Theorem 6.5); by a cited theorem, it holds the readings (Theorem 6.6). A symmetric operator with A−zA-zA−z and A−z‾A-\overline zA−z onto H\HilH for one non-real zzz is self-adjoint (Proposition 6.7).
  • Spectra by hand: a diagonal operator, and multiplication by a real continuous function, have the closure of their values as spectrum (Proposition 6.8).
  • Kinds of spectrum: point and continuous, discrete and essential (Definition 6.9); a point is essential exactly when it has an orthonormal Weyl sequence (Theorem 6.10, cited), and an interval of positive length inside the spectrum is essential.
  • The free resolvent has the kernel (m/ℏ2η)e−η∣x−y∣(m/\hbar^2\eta)e^{-\eta\abs{x-y}}(m/ℏ2η)e−η∣x−y∣ at z=−ℏ2η2/2mz=-\hbar^2\eta^2/2mz=−ℏ2η2/2m (Proposition 6.11), and the infinite chain has the band [c−2κ,c+2κ][c-2\kappa,c+2\kappa][c−2κ,c+2κ] and no eigenvalue (Proposition 6.12).
