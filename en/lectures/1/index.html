The particle in a box

What this lecture does

A chain with fixed ends suggests the energy rule for a particle in a box. We build the self-adjoint Hamiltonian from the sine basis and prove that its energy postulate determines it uniquely. Energy readings remain defined for states outside its domain. Its evolution is Lecture 2’s subject.

1.2 Wavefunctions in a box

The chain’s state was a vector of amplitudes, one for each site. A state of the particle assigns an amplitude ψ(x)\psi(x)ψ(x) to each position xxx in the box. The function ψ\psiψ is the wavefunction of the state. Lecture 2 postulates that ∣ψ(x)∣2\abs{\psi(x)}^2∣ψ(x)∣2 is the probability density for finding the particle near xxx. The wavefunctions form a vector space with the inner product ⟨φ,ψ⟩=∫0Lφ(x)‾ ψ(x) dx,\begin{equation} \label{eq:1:inner} \inner\varphi\psi=\int_0^L\overline{\varphi(x)}\,\psi(x)\,\dd x , \end{equation}⟨φ,ψ⟩=∫0L​φ(x)​ψ(x)dx,(1.3) which is antilinear in the first slot, as in Quantum Mechanics I. The space is L2(0,L)\Ltwo(0,L)L2(0,L). Its elements are the functions with ∫0L∣ψ∣2dx<∞\int_0^L\abs{\psi}^2\dd x<\infty∫0L​∣ψ∣2dx<∞, and two functions that differ only on a set of measure zero count as the same element (Linear Algebra, Def. 13.15). With the norm ∥ψ∥=⟨ψ,ψ⟩1/2\norm\psi=\inner\psi\psi^{1/2}∥ψ∥=⟨ψ,ψ⟩1/2, the space L2(0,L)\Ltwo(0,L)L2(0,L) is a Hilbert space, a complete inner product space.

So an element of L2(0,L)\Ltwo(0,L)L2(0,L) has no value at a single point. Changing ψ\psiψ at x=0x=0x=0 alone changes no integral, and it does not change the element. A condition such as ψ(0)=0\psi(0)=0ψ(0)=0 therefore cannot be imposed on the states. It will return as a condition on the domain of the Hamiltonian.

1.3 Stationary waves

The chain of §1.1 suggests Schrödinger’s rule: for a free particle of mass mmm, the energy acts on a wavefunction by ψ↦−(ℏ2/2m) ψ′′\psi\mapsto-(\hbar^2/2m)\,\psi''ψ↦−(ℏ2/2m)ψ′′. We take the rule as given here, and §1.5 makes it a postulate. A wavefunction of definite energy EEE, a stationary wave, solves −(ℏ2/2m) u′′=Eu-(\hbar^2/2m)\,u''=Eu−(ℏ2/2m)u′′=Eu. 1.4 Why the energy needs a domain

The energies grow without bound, En=n2E1E_n=n^2E_1En​=n2E1​, and this is where the new difficulty comes from. In the coefficients of (1.5), an energy that multiplies each stationary wave unu_nun​ by EnE_nEn​ multiplies each amplitude cnc_ncn​ by EnE_nEn​. For some states the result is not square-summable, so it is the coefficient sequence of no vector of L2(0,L)\Ltwo(0,L)L2(0,L). §1.7 gives such a state, with cn∝1/n2c_n\propto1/n^2cn​∝1/n2. The energy multiplies the unit vector unu_nun​ by EnE_nEn​, so it is not bounded. By the Hellinger–Toeplitz theorem of §0.1, it cannot be a symmetric operator defined on every vector.

Recall from Lecture 0 the operators with a domain (Definition 0.1), the adjoint A†\adj AA† of a densely defined operator AAA (Definition 0.2), and the symmetric and the self-adjoint operators (Definition 0.3). A symmetric operator AAA has D(A)⊆D(A†)\dom A\subseteq\dom{\adj A}D(A)⊆D(A†), with A†φ=Aφ\adj A\varphi=A\varphiA†φ=Aφ there, and it is self-adjoint when the two domains are equal.

Postulate 1.5 (Observables with an eigenbasis). The observables of a particle in the box are self-adjoint operators on L2(0,L)\Ltwo(0,L)L2(0,L). Suppose an observable AAA has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors e1,e2,…e_1,e_2,\dotse1​,e2​,…, with Aek=akekAe_k=a_ke_kAek​=ak​ek​. Then its readings are the numbers aka_kak​, and the measurement postulate of Quantum Mechanics I (Born’s and Lüders’ rules, Postulate 4.1) holds, with the projection EaE_aEa​ (not an energy) Eaψ=∑k: ak=a⟨ek,ψ⟩ ek,Prob⁡(a)=∥Eaψ∥2=∑k: ak=a∣⟨ek,ψ⟩∣2.\begin{equation} \label{eq:1:Ea} E_a\psi=\sum_{k:\,a_k=a}\inner{e_k}\psi\,e_k ,\qquad \Prob(a)=\norm{E_a\psi}^2=\sum_{k:\,a_k=a}\abs{\inner{e_k}\psi}^2 . \end{equation}Ea​ψ=k:ak​=a∑​⟨ek​,ψ⟩ek​,Prob(a)=∥Ea​ψ∥2=k:ak​=a∑​∣⟨ek​,ψ⟩∣2.(1.9)

By Parseval’s identity, the probabilities add to 111. This is where completeness matters. If the basis missed a direction, part of the probability would be lost.

The postulate asks for self-adjoint operators, not only symmetric ones, for two reasons that later lectures give. By the spectral theorem, which Lecture 5 cites, exactly the self-adjoint operators are integrals of spectral measures, the rules that give the probabilities of readings in intervals. The evolutions in time are strongly continuous unitary groups. Lecture 7 proves that every such group is generated by a self-adjoint operator divided by ℏ\hbarℏ, and only sketches the converse, that every self-adjoint operator divided by ℏ\hbarℏ generates such a group.

1.7 A state outside the domain

Some states lie outside the domain of HHH. Take the coefficients cn=90π2n2(n=1,2,3,… ).c_n=\frac{\sqrt{90}}{\pi^2n^2}\qquad(n=1,2,3,\dots).cn​=π2n290​​(n=1,2,3,…). Figure 1.2 draws this state beside the parabola of Example 1.9. Both curves are humps that vanish at both walls, and a look does not settle which of them lies in D(H)\dom HD(H). The coefficients do. For the parabola, n4cn2=960/π6n2n^4c_n^2=960/\pi^6n^2n4cn2​=960/π6n2 on the odd nnn, so the sum converges. For this state, every term of the sum is 90/π490/\pi^490/π4. Lecture 3 shows that every vector of D(H)\dom HD(H) has a continuous derivative on [0,L][0,L][0,L], which the infinite slope of this state at the left wall rules out.

The energy readings of ψ\psiψ are nevertheless defined, by the observables postulate. By Euler’s sum ∑n1/n2=π2/6\sum_n1/n^2=\pi^2/6∑n​1/n2=π2/6, the mean energy is ∑nEnProb⁡(En)=90π4 E1∑n1n2=15π2 E1≈1.52 E1,\sum_nE_n\Prob(E_n)=\frac{90}{\pi^4}\,E_1\sum_n\frac1{n^2} =\frac{15}{\pi^2}\,E_1\approx1.52\,E_1 ,n∑​En​Prob(En​)=π490​E1​n∑​n21​=π215​E1​≈1.52E1​, which is finite. The domain records a moment of the energy readings. For ψ∈D(H)\psi\in\dom Hψ∈D(H), Parseval’s identity gives ∥Hψ∥2=∑nEn2∣cn∣2=∑nEn2Prob⁡(En),\norm{H\psi}^2=\sum_nE_n^2\abs{c_n}^2=\sum_nE_n^2\Prob(E_n),∥Hψ∥2=n∑​En2​∣cn​∣2=n∑​En2​Prob(En​), the mean square of the energy readings. So a state lies in D(H)\dom HD(H) exactly when its energy readings have a finite mean square. For the state above the mean is finite and the mean square is not.

The rule alone is not self-adjoint. Symmetry alone does not make an observable. Physics texts often call an operator Hermitian when it is symmetric. In infinite dimension, symmetric and self-adjoint are different conditions. Integration by parts, as in the step on the rule, proves that Schrödinger’s rule on the twice continuously differentiable functions that vanish at the walls is symmetric. It is not self-adjoint there.

We show this by finding a vector of D(H)\dom HD(H) outside the domain of the rule. This vector is the function ψ(x)=2mℏ2{3Lx/8−x2/2for 0≤x≤L/2,L(L−x)/8for L/2≤x≤L.\psi(x)=\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\begin{cases}3Lx/8-x^2/2&\text{for }0\le x\le L/2,\\ L(L-x)/8&\text{for }L/2\le x\le L.\end{cases}ψ(x)=ℏ22m​{3Lx/8−x2/2L(L−x)/8​for 0≤x≤L/2,for L/2≤x≤L.​ To see this, note that this function vanishes at the walls, has a continuous derivative, and satisfies −(ℏ2/2m)ψ′′=g-(\hbar^2/2m)\psi''=g−(ℏ2/2m)ψ′′=g on each half of the box. If the rule were self-adjoint, it would obey the energy postulate, and by the uniqueness in Theorem 1.8 it would equal HHH. So it is not self-adjoint.

Summary

  • The open chain: with κ=ℏ2/2ma2\kappa=\hbar^2/2ma^2κ=ℏ2/2ma2, its levels 4κsin⁡2(nπ/2N)4\kappa\sin^2(n\pi/2N)4κsin2(nπ/2N) approach the box’s energies En=n2E1E_n=n^2E_1En​=n2E1​ at each fixed nnn as the sites come closer (Proposition 1.1).
  • The box Hamiltonian is self-adjoint on its domain, and its eigenvalues are the EnE_nEn​. It is the only self-adjoint operator that obeys the energy postulate (Theorem 1.8).
  • A state outside the domain: the domain holds the states whose energy readings have a finite mean square.
  • The rule alone is not self-adjoint: on the twice continuously differentiable functions that vanish at the walls, it is symmetric, and it is a proper restriction of HHH (§1.7).
