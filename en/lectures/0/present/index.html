Lecture 0

Mathematical preliminaries

An observable in infinite dimension may act only on a dense subspace. This lecture defines its domain, adjoint and self-adjointness. A diagonal example distinguishes symmetry from self-adjointness. Multiplication operators prepare the construction of position and momentum in Lecture 4.

0.1 Operators that cannot act on every vector

The observables of a particle need domains. Its position QQQ and momentum PPP should satisfy the canonical commutation relation QPψ−PQψ=iℏψQP\psi-PQ\psi=\ii\hbar\psiQPψ−PQψ=iℏψ for the vectors ψ\psiψ on which both sides are defined. By Quantum Mechanics I, Prop. 9.8, no pair of n×nn\times nn×n matrices does, since the trace of QP−PQQP-PQQP−PQ is 000 and the trace of iℏ11\ii\hbar\Idiℏ11 is iℏn\ii\hbar niℏn. In infinite dimension two cited theorems take over. They concern bounded operators and the self-adjoint ones among them (Linear Algebra, Defs 14.2 and 14.3).

By Moretti, Prop. 11.32, no two bounded self-adjoint operators QQQ and PPP on a Hilbert space satisfy QP−PQ=iℏ11QP-PQ=\ii\hbar\IdQP−PQ=iℏ11.

By the Hellinger–Toeplitz theorem (Teschl, Thm 2.9), an operator AAA that is defined on every vector and symmetric, that is, ⟨φ,Aψ⟩=⟨Aφ,ψ⟩\inner\varphi{A\psi}=\inner{A\varphi}\psi⟨φ,Aψ⟩=⟨Aφ,ψ⟩ for all φ\varphiφ and ψ\psiψ, is bounded. Observables are symmetric, as in Quantum Mechanics I. So QQQ and PPP cannot both be defined on every vector, and they satisfy the canonical relation only on part of the space (Lecture 4).

The energy of a particle in a box is another example. Lecture 1 shows that it multiplies a sequence of unit vectors by numbers that grow without bound. So it is not bounded, and by the Hellinger–Toeplitz theorem it cannot be a symmetric operator defined on every vector. In infinite dimension, an operator comes with the set of vectors it acts on.

0.2 Operators with domains

Definition 0.1 (Operator; densely defined). An operator AAA on a Hilbert space H\HilH is a linear map from a subspace D(A)⊆H\dom A\subseteq\HilD(A)⊆H, its domain, into H\HilH. It is densely defined if every vector of H\HilH is the limit of a sequence in D(A)\dom AD(A). Two operators are equal when they have the same domain and the same action on it.

Definition 0.2 (Adjoint). Let AAA be a densely defined operator on H\HilH. Its adjoint A†\adj AA† has the domain D(A†)={φ∈H: there is χ∈H with ⟨φ,Aψ⟩=⟨χ,ψ⟩ for every ψ∈D(A)},\begin{equation} \label{eq:0:adjdomain} \dom{\adj A}=\bigl\{\varphi\in\Hil:\ \text{there is }\chi\in\Hil\text{ with } \inner\varphi{A\psi}=\inner\chi\psi\text{ for every }\psi\in\dom A\bigr\}, \end{equation}D(A†)={φ∈H: there is χ∈H with ⟨φ,Aψ⟩=⟨χ,ψ⟩ for every ψ∈D(A)},(0.1) and on it A†φ=χ\adj A\varphi=\chiA†φ=χ.

The density of the domain makes χ\chiχ unique. Suppose that χ1\chi_1χ1​ and χ2\chi_2χ2​ both satisfy the condition in (0.1), and let v=χ1−χ2v=\chi_1-\chi_2v=χ1​−χ2​. Then ⟨v,ψ⟩=0\inner v\psi=0⟨v,ψ⟩=0 for every ψ∈D(A)\psi\in\dom Aψ∈D(A). Since D(A)\dom AD(A) is dense, there is a sequence ψj\psi_jψj​ in D(A)\dom AD(A) with ψj→v\psi_j\to vψj​→v. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, ∥v∥2=∣⟨v,v⟩−⟨v,ψj⟩∣=∣⟨v,v−ψj⟩∣≤∥v∥ ∥v−ψj∥⟶0.\norm v^2=\abs{\inner vv-\inner v{\psi_j}}=\abs{\inner v{v-\psi_j}} \le\norm v\,\norm{v-\psi_j}\longrightarrow0 .∥v∥2=∣⟨v,v⟩−⟨v,ψj​⟩∣=∣⟨v,v−ψj​⟩∣≤∥v∥∥v−ψj​∥⟶0. So v=0v=0v=0, and χ1=χ2\chi_1=\chi_2χ1​=χ2​. This is where the definition needs AAA to be densely defined.

The adjoint is an operator. Indeed, let φ1\varphi_1φ1​ and φ2\varphi_2φ2​ lie in D(A†)\dom{\adj A}D(A†), with the vectors χ1\chi_1χ1​ and χ2\chi_2χ2​, and let aaa and bbb be numbers. Since the inner product is antilinear in its first slot, ⟨aφ1+bφ2,Aψ⟩=a‾ ⟨χ1,ψ⟩+b‾ ⟨χ2,ψ⟩=⟨aχ1+bχ2,ψ⟩for every ψ∈D(A).\inner{a\varphi_1+b\varphi_2}{A\psi} =\overline a\,\inner{\chi_1}\psi+\overline b\,\inner{\chi_2}\psi =\inner{a\chi_1+b\chi_2}\psi \quad\text{for every }\psi\in\dom A .⟨aφ1​+bφ2​,Aψ⟩=a⟨χ1​,ψ⟩+b⟨χ2​,ψ⟩=⟨aχ1​+bχ2​,ψ⟩for every ψ∈D(A). So aφ1+bφ2a\varphi_1+b\varphi_2aφ1​+bφ2​ lies in D(A†)\dom{\adj A}D(A†), and A†(aφ1+bφ2)=aχ1+bχ2\adj A(a\varphi_1+b\varphi_2)=a\chi_1+b\chi_2A†(aφ1​+bφ2​)=aχ1​+bχ2​.

On Cn\C^nCn the adjoint is the conjugate transpose. More generally, if AAA is bounded and D(A)=H\dom A=\HilD(A)=H, the adjoint of Linear Algebra, Def. 14.3, gives a vector χ\chiχ for every φ\varphiφ, and A†\adj AA† is that adjoint. For other operators χ\chiχ need not exist for every φ\varphiφ, and D(A†)\dom{\adj A}D(A†) records the φ\varphiφ for which it does.

Definition 0.3 (Symmetric; self-adjoint). A densely defined operator AAA is symmetric if ⟨φ,Aψ⟩=⟨Aφ,ψ⟩\inner\varphi{A\psi}=\inner{A\varphi}\psi⟨φ,Aψ⟩=⟨Aφ,ψ⟩ for all φ,ψ∈D(A)\varphi,\psi\in\dom Aφ,ψ∈D(A). It is self-adjoint if A†=A\adj A=AA†=A, that is, if D(A†)=D(A)\dom{\adj A}=\dom AD(A†)=D(A) and A†φ=Aφ\adj A\varphi=A\varphiA†φ=Aφ there.

If AAA is symmetric and φ∈D(A)\varphi\in\dom Aφ∈D(A), then χ=Aφ\chi=A\varphiχ=Aφ satisfies the condition in (0.1). So D(A)⊆D(A†)\dom A\subseteq\dom{\adj A}D(A)⊆D(A†), with A†φ=Aφ\adj A\varphi=A\varphiA†φ=Aφ there. Self-adjointness asks in addition that the adjoint have no larger domain. On Cn\C^nCn the two notions coincide, and both are the self-adjoint matrices of Quantum Mechanics I.

The adjoint of a symmetric operator contains the operator. Containing is a relation between operators that the course uses often.

Definition 0.4 (Extension). Let AAA and BBB be operators on H\HilH. We write A⊆BA\subseteq BA⊆B, and say that BBB extends AAA, when D(A)⊆D(B)andBψ=Aψfor every ψ∈D(A).\dom A\subseteq\dom B\quad\text{and}\quad B\psi=A\psi\quad\text{for every }\psi\in\dom A .D(A)⊆D(B)andBψ=Aψfor every ψ∈D(A).

So a symmetric AAA satisfies A⊆A†A\subseteq\adj AA⊆A†, and it is self-adjoint when also A†⊆A\adj A\subseteq AA†⊆A.

Proposition 0.5 (Adjoints reverse inclusions). Let AAA and BBB be densely defined operators with A⊆BA\subseteq BA⊆B. Then B†⊆A†\adj B\subseteq\adj AB†⊆A†.

Proof. Let φ∈D(B†)\varphi\in\dom{\adj B}φ∈D(B†), with χ=B†φ\chi=\adj B\varphiχ=B†φ. Then ⟨φ,Bψ⟩=⟨χ,ψ⟩\inner\varphi{B\psi}=\inner\chi\psi⟨φ,Bψ⟩=⟨χ,ψ⟩ for every ψ∈D(B)\psi\in\dom Bψ∈D(B). Every ψ∈D(A)\psi\in\dom Aψ∈D(A) lies in D(B)\dom BD(B), with Bψ=AψB\psi=A\psiBψ=Aψ. So ⟨φ,Aψ⟩=⟨χ,ψ⟩\inner\varphi{A\psi}=\inner\chi\psi⟨φ,Aψ⟩=⟨χ,ψ⟩ for every ψ∈D(A)\psi\in\dom Aψ∈D(A). Hence φ∈D(A†)\varphi\in\dom{\adj A}φ∈D(A†), and A†φ=χ=B†φ\adj A\varphi=\chi=\adj B\varphiA†φ=χ=B†φ. 

A larger operator has to satisfy the condition (0.1) for more vectors ψ\psiψ, so its adjoint has a smaller domain. The following example shows a symmetric operator whose adjoint is strictly larger.

0.3 How this course proves

Every result of this course carries one of three tags. A result with no tag is proved in full. A result tagged sketched has its central idea proved and routine details omitted, or it is proved with the help of a cited theorem. A result tagged cited is taken from a named source, with its page.

A few facts of analysis are used throughout. Linear Algebra states them without proof, and this course takes them as standing facts:

S1

Plancherel: the Fourier transform is unitary on L2(Rn)\Ltwo(\R^n)L2(Rn), with its inversion formula (Lecture 4).

S2

The Fourier basis e2πikx/L/Le^{2\pi\ii kx/L}/\sqrt Le2πikx/L/L​ (k∈Zk\in\Zk∈Z) and the sine basis 2/L sin⁡(nπx/L)\sqrt{2/L}\,\sin(n\pi x/L)2/L​sin(nπx/L) (n≥1n\ge1n≥1) of L2(0,L)\Ltwo(0,L)L2(0,L) are complete (Lecture 1 and Lecture 3).

S3

Products of orthonormal bases are orthonormal bases of L2(R2)\Ltwo(\R^2)L2(R2) and L2(R3)\Ltwo(\R^3)L2(R3) (Lecture 13).

S4

The smooth functions that vanish outside a closed bounded set inside the interval, or inside Rn\R^nRn, are dense in L2\LtwoL2 of the interval, or of Rn\R^nRn. So are the rapidly decreasing functions of Lecture 4.

A result whose only unproved ingredients are standing facts counts as proved.

0.4 Multiplication operators

The diagonal operator of Example 0.6 multiplies the nnnth entry of a sequence by nnn. Its counterpart on a line multiplies a function by a function, and the position of a particle on a line multiplies by xxx (Lecture 4). The space L2(R)\Ltwo(\R)L2(R) is defined in the same way as L2\LtwoL2 of an interval (Linear Algebra, Def. 13.15): its vectors are classes of square-integrable functions on R\RR, and two functions lie in the same class when they agree outside a null set. For a bounded real multiplier on an interval, Linear Algebra, Ex. 14.6, shows that multiplication is a bounded self-adjoint operator. An unbounded multiplier, such as xxx on R\RR, needs a domain.

Let ggg be a real continuous function on R\RR, and let MgM_gMg​ act by (Mgψ)(x)=g(x)ψ(x)(M_g\psi)(x)=g(x)\psi(x)(Mg​ψ)(x)=g(x)ψ(x) on D(Mg)={ψ: gψ∈L2(R)}\dom{M_g}=\{\psi:\ g\psi\in\Ltwo(\R)\}D(Mg​)={ψ: gψ∈L2(R)}. Summary

  • Domains are needed: a symmetric operator defined on every vector is bounded. The position and the momentum of a particle cannot both be bounded, and the energy of a particle in a box is not (§0.1).
  • An operator comes with its domain, and two operators are equal only when their domains are (Definition 0.1).
  • The adjoint of a densely defined operator is defined on the vectors φ\varphiφ for which some χ\chiχ satisfies ⟨φ,Aψ⟩=⟨χ,ψ⟩\inner\varphi{A\psi}=\inner\chi\psi⟨φ,Aψ⟩=⟨χ,ψ⟩ for every ψ∈D(A)\psi\in\dom Aψ∈D(A) (Definition 0.2).
  • A symmetric operator AAA satisfies A⊆A†A\subseteq\adj AA⊆A†. It is self-adjoint when the two are equal (Definition 0.3), and adjoints reverse inclusions (Proposition 0.5). The diagonal operator on finite sequences is symmetric, and its adjoint is larger (Example 0.6).
  • Multiplication by a real continuous function, on the vectors it keeps square-integrable, is self-adjoint, and a value taken at only finitely many points is not an eigenvalue. A unitary equivalence keeps both (Lemmas 0.7 and 0.8).

Lecture 1 uses these definitions for the energy of a particle in a box, and Lecture 4 uses the multiplication operators for a particle on a line.
